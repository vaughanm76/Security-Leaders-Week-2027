      The rule base keeps growing. Implementation stopped following it.

      Annex 17 has been amended nineteen times and reissued in twelve editions, and the guidance manual behind it thirteen times. Global effective implementation climbed steeply through to 2017 — and has moved six-tenths of a point in the eight years since, across three further amendments of Annex 17 and three new editions of Doc 8973. What that gap exposes is not a shortage of standards. It is the capacity to manage security against them.

      +18.0
      Percentage points of global effective implementation gained between 2008 and 2017.
      +0.6
      Points moved between 2017 and 2025, across three amendments of Annex 17 and three editions of Doc 8973.
      ≈72%
      The level global effective implementation has held at for the better part of a decade.
      The binding constraint is capability, not coverage More prescription is no longer converting into more implementation. A security management system is what turns a standard into something an organisation can actually run, evidence and improve.

      Read this carefully before using it. USAP‑CMA effective implementation measures a State's oversight capability, not an operator's security outcomes. The protocol question set also expands as the Annex grows, so States are re-measured against a harder test each cycle — holding roughly 72% against a rising bar is not the same thing as standing still.

      That qualification arguably reinforces the point rather than undermining it: the yardstick keeps moving because the rule base keeps growing, faster than States and operators can absorb it.

      Two routes. Only one of them moved.

      SeMS was proposed to the ICAO AVSEC Panel as a standard and did not get there. What happened instead is that the industry built it — through IOSA, through manuals, training and certification, and through a community that now runs to four figures. Twenty-four years of that history, split by which route each step took.

      ICAO route Standard-setting through Annex 17
      Industry route IOSA, IATA guidance, tools and community
      One binding step in twenty-four years, and it came from IOSA 2007 made SeMS an ISM standard for airlines. The 2024 extension to service providers is a recommended practice, so the tier that performs most of the measures is addressed without being bound. Nothing here was ever inherited down the regulatory cascade the way the mandated baseline is — which is why SeMS maturity varies so widely by operator type. IATA is not alone on this route: ACI's SeMS Handbook takes the same concept to airports, so the two industry bodies now cover both ends of the chain.

      Rule and risk, inside one structure

      A SeMS does not replace the mandated baseline. It holds the baseline and the risk-based measures in the same structure, so that both answer to one accountable outcome — and so that assurance evidence feeds back into how oversight is targeted.

      Oversight

      Who checks whom

      Each tier verifies the one below it, with depth set by risk rather than by the calendar.

      • ICAO USAP audits the State
      • State oversees the operator
      • Operator oversees its service providers
      Rule-based

      The mandated baseline

      Non-negotiable, inherited downward, and identical for everyone in the same category.

      • Annex 17 SARPs
      • Regional regulations, where they apply
      • National regulations and directives
      • Operator programme — AOSP / SSP
      Risk-based

      Measures beyond the baseline

      Threat-informed, specific to your operation, and revised when the picture changes.

      • Threat and vulnerability assessment
      • Measures proportionate to the risk identified
      • Assurance monitoring and SePIs

      Verifying what you don't control

      The cascade makes each tier accountable for outcomes the tier below it actually delivers — usually in another company, often in another jurisdiction. Today that verification runs on assertion, paper and repeat audit. Digital trust is the mechanism that lets the evidence cross those boundaries and be checked independently, without a system integration or a bilateral arrangement on either side.

      What it proves

      Provenance, not much else

      Three things, cleanly and instantly:

      • The approval is genuine and unaltered
      • It is bound to one exact document version
      • It has not been superseded or revoked
      What it does not prove

      That the programme is any good

      A cryptographically verifiable approval of a weak AOSP is still a weak AOSP. Quality comes from the security management system behind the document. The attestation only removes doubt about where the document came from and whether it still stands.

      What it removes

      Verification done twice

      • The same fact re-established at every crossing
      • Bilateral system integration between parties
      • Paper that has to be trusted because it cannot be checked

      Where it applies, in order

      Working now

      Approvals

      AOSP and SSP letters of approval, between an authority and an operator. Bounded, already mandatory, and already painful — which is why it is the right place to start rather than the whole of the ambition.

      Highest value

      Service delivery

      SeMS status and certification for external service providers. An operator with two hundred outstations cannot audit them all; verifiable evidence is what makes risk-based oversight defensible rather than aspirational. This is where the accountability gap is widest.

      Further out

      Recognition

      One-Stop Security decisions, assessor credentials and competency records — moving State-to-State recognition off paper and bilateral negotiation, and onto evidence a receiving State can check for itself.

      The assurance rail, made to work across boundaries The Aviation Security Trust Framework is the working implementation of this — verifiable AOSP and SSP approvals, anchored in open standards so that no participant depends on a single provider. astf.iata.org

      The hard part is governance, not cryptography. Who is entitled to issue, who maintains that list, and how revocation is decided are political questions rather than technical ones. A neutral, state-operated anchor answers them well enough for a demonstrator; it does not answer them for a global framework.

      Closing that gap is a governance task for the industry and the States that choose to take part, not a case for a new Annex 17 provision: the SeMS key concepts have sat in the Annex since 2011, and the value lies in how they are implemented, not in re-legislating them.
